# YellowBook Problem: The Missing Layer of “Friend Plans”

## The Core Problem

Most people don’t struggle to make friends — they struggle to **sustain friendships when life gets busy**.

A big reason is surprisingly mundane: **informal plans are hard to hold onto**.

Friendship is made of small, human commitments — *“let’s do lunch,” “gym Monday,” “hang Saturday,” “call sometime this week.”*  
These plans matter emotionally, but they’re often **too soft, too social, and too context-heavy** for the tools we’re supposed to use.

So they slip.

And when they slip, people don’t just lose a plan — they lose momentum, warmth, and connection.

---

## The “Friend Plans” Gap

There’s a missing layer between:

- **Chats** (where plans are casually made, then buried)
- **Calendars** (where plans are rigid, global, and mixed with everything)
- **Invites / event apps** (where plans become formal, hosted, and RSVP-shaped)

Most real friendship plans don’t fit any of those categories.

They’re *not* just time-management objects.  
They’re *not* events that need a production.  
They’re *not* meant to become a public performance.

They’re simply: **social intent that deserves follow-through**.

---

## What Makes These Plans Hard

### 1) They’re informal, but meaningful  
Many plans are “promise-shaped” without being official.

- “Let’s grab lunch Thursday.”
- “Want to lift Monday?”
- “Let’s catch up this weekend.”

They often have no final time, no firm location, and no clear “host.”  
But they still represent care. Dropping them can feel like dropping the person.

### 2) They carry relationship context, not just logistics  
The plan isn’t only *when* — it’s *who* and *why*.

- “Sam’s been stressed — the gym helps.”
- “Bob just started a new job — lunch is a check-in.”
- “Dave and I haven’t talked in a month — a Saturday hang matters.”

Most tools don’t hold this kind of context well, and people don’t want to write it into a calendar title.

### 3) They’re easy to lose in the places they’re created  
They’re typically made in texts, DMs, or hallway moments — and then:

- the chat moves on
- the message gets buried
- the “we should” never becomes “we did”

This creates a quiet failure mode: *you meant to, but didn’t.*

### 4) The follow-through steps are the real friction  
The hard part usually isn’t scheduling — it’s everything around it:

- remembering
- checking if it’s still on
- suggesting specifics without awkwardness
- rescheduling without making it weird
- keeping momentum without being pushy

When people avoid that friction, plans dissolve by default.

### 5) Current tools add the wrong kind of weight  
**Calendars** are powerful, but often feel too heavy and too global:
- mixed with work, obligations, appointments
- optimized for precision and time blocks
- not emotionally designed for friendship
- easy for friend-plans to get lost or feel “too much”

**Invite/event apps** handle coordination well, but often feel too official:
- requires a “real event” frame
- implies formality, hosting, and RSVP theater
- can make casual plans feel inflated or performative

In both cases, the tool shape changes the social moment — and not in a way that fits everyday friendship.

---

## The Human Cost

When informal plans evaporate, people often interpret it personally:

- “I guess we’re not that close anymore.”
- “They didn’t follow through.”
- “I keep meaning to reach out, but I don’t.”
- “It’s been too long — now it’s awkward.”

The result isn’t just disorganization. It’s **drift**.

And drift is how many adult friendships fade — not from conflict, but from friction and forgetting.

---

## What This Problem Is *Not*

This is not primarily a problem of:
- productivity
- optimization
- maximizing social output
- “managing people”
- tracking relationship health

It’s a problem of **holding warmth and intention through busy life**.

The unmet need is simple:

> A way to keep informal social commitments from disappearing —  
> without turning them into calendar bureaucracy or formal invitations.

---

## A Plain-Language Summary

People have a lot of small plans with people they care about.  
Those plans are easy to lose, awkward to maintain, and hard to follow through on.  
Existing tools are either too heavy (calendar) or too formal (invites).  
So friendships quietly drift — not because people don’t care, but because the “friend plans” layer has no natural home.
